One cache per node; each tree describes local data

Requester and responder name the roles in an exchange. They are not different kinds of tree. A node uses the same cache of resident trees when it fetches from a peer, keeps a live query current, or answers a peer’s comparison.

The node asks whether a cached tree can serve the Selection. The tree must contain every required member, and its key layout must express the requested range and any ordering a LIMIT needs. Readers share a tree only when their effective access is equal: the policy restricts their queries the same way and lets them retrieve the same entities, and either they hold the same credentials or each holds a fresh policy proof that it may read every entity state. The node checks this when a reader takes the tree and at every snapshot; where per-entity read checks could differ between readers, each gets its own tree. Each snapshot reflects every commit the node has published.

One resident tree can serve several query scopes. A tree covering every match for project = 'acme' AND foo > 7, keyed by project and foo, also serves foo > 10 within that project. It cannot serve foo > 3. Compatible ORDER BY and LIMIT changes select a view from that complete tree; they do not change its TreeSpec or truncate its membership. If the keys cannot determine the requested limited ordering, the query uses its own bounded tree. This also applies to descending limited views when the predicate may admit missing sort values. The bounded tree uses storage to refill its membership when changes cross the limit. Snapshots retain only metadata: keys, IDs, Clocks and digests. Entity payloads remain in the resident entity or storage engine.

A cold limited query keeps a bounded result. If no complete tree can serve ORDER BY foo LIMIT 10, the node reads and retains only that result. The cache records limited coverage separately from TreeSpec. This entry can serve the same Selection with compatible access and key layout; it cannot stand in for an unlimited or differently limited query. When a change can affect its result, a validated bounded read finds replacements. Complete and limited entries use the same cache and RAII ownership.

Each resident tree owns a reactor maintenance subscription and folds in committed changes even when idle. It subscribes before its admission read and folds in the changes that crossed the read before anything uses the tree. Its bounded queue holds key and head metadata, never entity payloads or entity handles; a tree that falls behind fails explicitly, and the next snapshot rebuilds it. A snapshot pins its tree while the snapshot lives, through an RAII lease. Nothing pins a tree between snapshots: an idle tree stays in the LRU, still maintained, until the idle allowance of 32 trees and 128 MiB of accounted metadata and tree memory evicts the least recently used. A query whose tree was evicted rebuilds it from storage at its next snapshot, so a busy cache can make a live query’s periodic check rebuild its tree. Eviction never deletes stored entities. A resident tree builds its digests the first time something reads one and maintains them on every commit after that; while it is read only as head inventories, it builds none. A snapshot’s digests agree with the entries it froze; a partial view never replaces the resident’s complete digest.

What the tree-related types mean

TypeMeaning
TreeSpecA tree’s membership predicate and canonical key layout.
TreeSpanAn exact interval of full tree addresses. The endpoints include the key bytes; no external prefix is prepended to interpret them.
TreeSpanDigestA span, its aggregate digest and count, plus a containing subtree prefix used for refinement.
EntityHeadAn entity ID and its full causal clock. In SyncEntities and EntitiesSynced, an empty clock means the sender holds no state of the entity.
KeyedEntityHeadAn EntityHead plus this tree’s property-key bytes. The ID distinguishes entities with equal keys.
TreeSpanInventoryA span and every matching entity head within it. Empty heads explicitly mean no matches within those bounds.
SpanContentEither Heads(TreeSpanInventory) or Digest(TreeSpanDigest).
KnownStateA node’s account of its query result under a TreeSpec: disjoint spans, each described by a digest or an inventory.
SpanQuestionOne CompareSpans question: Digest(TreeSpan) asks for the responder’s digest of exactly that span, and Heads(TreeSpan) for one page of its heads from the span’s start.
SpanAnswerOne SpansCompared answer, in its question’s place: Digest(TreeSpanDigest) or Heads(TreeSpanInventory).

The requester offers a KnownState of its copy in Fetch and SubscribeQuery, and the responder answers with its own, the target, in the Fetch response or in QuerySubscribed. An empty inventory says “no matches here.” A range no span covers says “unknown.” Neither says anything about entity state a node holds outside the query’s result.

A node lists every head in one inventory when its result fits an offer’s limits; otherwise it describes the result as a bounded frontier of span digests. The protocol accepts disjoint mixtures too. An offer’s inventory may hold more heads than a follow-up page.

Inventory encoding and crate ownership

Inventories use PackedHeads. Sorted key prefixes can be encoded once and reused by suffixes in a flat buffer; plain encoding is used when smaller. Borrowed cursors read the packed heads. The walkthrough expands them for readability.

TreeSpec, TreeSpan and TreeSpanDigest belong to digest-tree. Entity clocks, inventories, KnownState and the span questions and answers belong to proto because they describe peer knowledge with actual clocks. The standalone tree works with head hashes and does not manage network exchanges.

Each request has its own request ID and gets exactly one response. Updates travel on the per-peer update channel, the one that carries a subscription’s live changes. A comparison’s updates can arrive before, between or after the responses around them, in any order, and one can be lost. A node admits a peer’s updates only while it holds a subscription with that peer or has a Fetch in flight with it, and applies them under those queries’ credentials.

Questions, answers and failure paths

The requester asks every CompareSpans question about the tree its target’s spec names. A digest answer covers exactly its question’s span. A heads answer starts where its question’s span starts and ends where the span ends or, stopping short, at its own last head; the requester then asks for the rest. The requester checks every answer against its question before using it. CompareSpans and SyncEntities carry the Selection but no query ID: the responder answers from the Selection, the spec and the request’s own credentials, so a responder that kept nothing since the query opened can answer them.

When no tree under the requester’s TreeSpec can serve the Selection on the responder, as when two catalogs type a key part differently, the responder answers under a tree keyed by entity ID alone, which every catalog can serve. It treats the offer as unknown and pushes every member, and the requester checks readiness under the target’s spec. A node that cannot take a stable snapshot while its catalog or data keep changing tries again, up to eight snapshots, before the request fails.

A failed request returns Error, which the requester reports as a server error. A request that opens a query waits for its response as long as it takes; each later comparison request waits at most the current idle interval, and one that gets no answer in time leaves its repair without progress. A Fetch that makes no progress through three repairs fails with SyncStalled, naming the peer. A live query that stalls keeps its local data, goes back to pending, and subscribes again on the relay’s five-second retry timer. A failure ends only the query it concerns; the connection and its other queries continue.

Membership as of the snapshot, entity data as it is now

The target’s heads say which entities were in the responder’s result when it took its snapshot, and at which Clocks. The responder drops that snapshot before it pushes anything. Its push task reads each entity’s current state from the resident entity or storage, a page at a time as it sends, and checks the reader’s access again; the tree keeps no entity data.

So a push can carry a newer state than the target lists. The requester covers a target head when its copy holds that head or a causally newer one, so a newer push, or a local write the responder has not seen, still counts. When the requester holds an earlier head of an entity, the responder sends the events from that head to the current state if they are smaller than the state; otherwise it sends the state.

A live update carries the entity’s state with the events that made it. Each update item’s predicate_relevance records how membership changed on the responder: (query_id, Initial) for a subscription’s initial data, Add or Remove for a live change that moved the entity into or out of the result, and nothing for a change within the result or for the pushes of a Fetch or a repair. The requester’s applier does not read these tags; its own reactor decides what each applied change means for its queries.

When a query is ready, and how it repairs

A query is ready when its copy matches or exceeds the target:

  1. every responder head the requester has seen, in the target or in a page CompareSpans returned, is covered by its copy at that head or a causally newer one, except the entities the responder reported unavailable; a span whose digest equals the requester’s own digest over the same bounds counts as covered; and
  2. every member of the requester’s own result that the responder’s account does not list is settled: the responder reported it unavailable, or the copy covers the head EntitiesSynced reported for it.

The requester judges progress by its own copy alone, never by what a peer says it sent: progress means its tree changed or its uncovered heads shrank. Where the responder pushed nothing, in spans the offer described by digest, the requester repairs at once. Otherwise, when the copy has gone the idle interval without progress and is not ready, it repairs, doubling the interval each time.

A repair descends on the requester. Over a differing span it refines its own tree and asks CompareSpans for the responder’s digests of exactly the child bounds, and it asks for a page of heads where a span counts at most a page of the responder’s entries or its own tree cannot split it. It checks each head against its copy and names in SyncEntities what it lacks or holds older, together with its own members that the pages do not list. The data arrives as updates.

A live query becomes established when it is ready, not when QuerySubscribed arrives. From then on it checks itself with one CompareSpans holding a single question about the whole tree: its heads when the copy fits one page, otherwise its digest. A check that disagrees right after another one repairs the copy, since updates still in flight can explain one disagreement but not two.

Both peers enforce the protocol’s limits

The protocol fixes the limits below in its limits module. Each peer sizes what it sends to fit them and refuses whatever it receives that exceeds them, so a peer that changed one alone would have its messages refused. Byte limits count a message’s work: its encoded bytes plus the bytes its inventories saved by sharing key prefixes.

  • Offer or target (KnownState): at most 2,048 heads, 128 spans and 256 KiB of work. A node lists every head when they fit; otherwise it describes its result as a frontier of span digests, which by its own choice stays within 16 KiB and 32 refinements.
  • Heads page: at most 128 heads and 64 KiB of work.
  • CompareSpans: at most 128 questions, of which at most 4 ask for heads, so that its answers stay within 256 KiB of work.
  • SyncEntities: at most 128 entities.

The responder sizes its updates by its own choice: at most 128 items and about 1 MiB of item data each, and at least one item. Updates have no flow control of their own. Either peer can change its own choices without breaking the other. None of these numbers is a performance guarantee.

Read alongside the implementation

Ankurah keeps reactor-maintained, metadata-only resident trees in one node-owned LRU, with compatible selection reuse, bounded coverage and RAII ownership. Trees are held in memory; storage-backed trees are not implemented. Message examples are schematic and message boundaries are illustrative.
